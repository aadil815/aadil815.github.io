A Long Island water story

The water beneath us remembers what we put down the drain.

Everyday products and historic industrial pollution can affect the same groundwater that Long Island communities rely on; treatment and infrastructure decisions can make protection more expensive. This is the story of what was found, what it costs residents, and how public pressure helped turn evidence into policy.

01 / The hidden pathway

The label tells you what a product is for. It may not tell you what it leaves behind.

1,4-dioxane is often an unwanted manufacturing byproduct, not an ingredient intentionally added to make a product work. When products are washed down the drain, that byproduct can become part of the water story.

“

Long Island's groundwater is its drinking-water supply. That makes source control personal: the things that leave our homes can eventually meet the aquifer.

Research synthesis from CCE reports and Doherty et al. (2022)

There is no single source for every detection. Historic industrial releases, wastewater, septic systems, and consumer products can all matter. The important distinction is that product contamination is a source that can be reduced before it enters the water cycle.

Some ordinary products carried surprisingly high levels.

04 / From evidence to law

Policy moved in layers: product rules, water standards, waste reduction, and money for infrastructure.

Research helped open several doors, and each addresses a different part of the problem.

2019–25
Enacted · CCE campaign

Senate Bill S4389B: 1,4-dioxane product limits

Signed by the Governor in 2019, the law limits 1,4-dioxane in household cleansing, personal-care, and cosmetic products. The product standard phased down to 2,000 ppb in 2023 and 1,000 ppb in 2024; CCE's 2026 case study describes full implementation in 2025.

Read the bill ↗
2020
Enacted · PFAS source control

S8817 / A4739-C: PFAS in food packaging

New York banned intentionally added PFAS in certain direct-contact food packaging. It took effect December 31, 2022.

Read the bill ↗
2020
Enacted · statewide waste reduction

New York State Bag Waste Reduction Act

Officially called the New York State Bag Waste Reduction Act (ECL Article 27, Title 28), this law took effect March 1, 2020 and prohibits people required to collect New York State sales tax from distributing plastic carryout bags, subject to statutory exemptions. It addresses bag waste, not 1,4-dioxane or PFAS.

See NYSDEC guidance ↗
2020
Enacted · drinking-water standards

New York sets limits for emerging contaminants

New York established public-water maximum contaminant levels of 1 ppb for 1,4-dioxane and 10 ppt each for PFOA and PFOS.

Read the DOH standards ↗
2022
Enacted · funding

S8008C and the $4.2 billion Environmental Bond Act

The signed budget bill included the Environmental Bond Act. Voters approved it, including at least $650 million for water-quality improvement and resilient infrastructure. CCE supported the campaign.

S8008C ↗CCE campaign ↗
2022
Enacted · packaging and waste

Expanded polystyrene ban begins

New York's statewide ban on covered expanded-polystyrene food containers and loose-fill packaging began January 1, 2022.

Read the law ↗
2021–24
Proposed · not enacted

S8364A: PFAS in cosmetics and personal-care products

This proposal would prohibit intentionally added PFAS in cosmetics and personal-care products. It remains proposed, not enacted.

Read the proposal ↗
2025
Enacted · phased implementation

PFAS restrictions in apparel begin

New York's PFAS-in-apparel law prohibits intentionally added PFAS in new apparel after January 1, 2025; outdoor apparel for severe wet conditions has a later January 1, 2028 phase-in.

See NYSDEC guidance ↗
2024–26
Implementation · CCE follow-up

Testing shows reformulation can work

NYSDEC adopted final 1,4-dioxane product regulations in September 2024. CCE's January 2024 follow-up testing showed major reductions in five re-tested detergents, and its 2026 case study reports that the product law was fully implemented in 2025.

Read the regulation notice ↗

Local Law No. 4-2008 created Nassau's Plastic Bag Reduction and Recycling Law for covered stores and chains, taking effect November 27, 2008.

05 / The measurable result

The most hopeful part of this story is that the chemistry changed.

CCE re-tested five detergents that had appeared among the higher results in the original product testing.

ProductEarlier result2024 resultReduction
Tide Original14,000 ppb205 ppb98.5%
Persil6,100 ppb928 ppb84.8%
Tide Simply Oxi8,300 ppb567 ppb93.2%
Gain Original10,000 ppb188 ppb98.1%
Dreft Stage 1 / Newborn10,000 ppb127 ppb98.7%
95.8%
combined measured reduction

Calculated from the combined before-and-after concentrations for the five detergents re-tested by CCE: 48,400 ppb combined earlier results versus 2,015 ppb in 2024. Manufacturers could reformulate while continuing to sell effective products.

What this does not mean

Product reformulation can reduce new product-related contamination; it does not erase historic groundwater plumes or eliminate PFAS. Water suppliers still need monitoring, treatment, and infrastructure funding.
