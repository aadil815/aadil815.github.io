Genetics & Society Research Report

Islamic Bioethics in Modern Medicine

A two-part research synthesis on organ donation and assisted reproduction within Islam. The report examines how medical innovation intersects with preservation of life, prevention of harm, bodily dignity, family formation, and lineage.

Organ transplantation Assisted reproduction Islamic jurisprudence Health communication

Research focus

The supplied papers do not reduce Islamic bioethics to a single verdict. They show a decision-making framework: seek knowledge, weigh benefit against harm, protect dignity, clarify consent, and preserve family lineage. Interpretations vary across scholars, sects, national systems, and clinical circumstances.

This is an educational synthesis of 2021 coursework and its cited literature. It is not a religious ruling, medical advice, or a substitute for consultation with qualified clinicians and religious authorities.

Report frame

A values-based approach to medical innovation

Across both papers, the ethical analysis is shaped by several recurring considerations. These lenses explain why the same technology may be accepted, limited, debated, or prohibited depending on the circumstances.

Lens 01

Preservation of life

Saving a life carries substantial ethical weight, particularly when transplantation or treatment offers a necessary path to survival.

Lens 02

Prevention of harm

Benefit is weighed against harm to the donor, recipient, embryo, family, and wider community.

Lens 03

Dignity and lineage

Bodily dignity shapes donation debates. Family lineage shapes assisted-reproduction debates.

01 / Organ transplant

Destigmatizing donation and transplantation

The first paper analyzes organ donation as a medical necessity and a communication challenge. Living donation is often treated more clearly than deceased donation, where debates about death determination, bodily sanctity, burial practices, and consent become central.

Why the subject matters

Transplantation can become necessary when organs such as the kidney, liver, heart, or lung fail. A compatible donor-recipient match is required, while the need for donated organs exceeds availability.

  • Paired or regenerative donations may be allowed when the donor does not face significant harm.
  • Removing a vital organ from a living donor is prohibited.
  • Buying and selling organs is prohibited in the rulings summarized by the paper.

Where disagreement emerges

The paper identifies deceased donation as the more contested area. The underlying questions include whether neurologically determined death is sufficient, how quickly burial should occur, how consent should work, and how the body should be treated after death.

The source paper presents three broad views: donation is impermissible; donation is generally impermissible but may become permissible in certain circumstances; or donation is permitted when specific conditions are met.
01A / Organ transplant

From ethical ruling to clinical reality

The paper moves beyond abstract permissibility. It argues that healthcare workers, religious leaders, governments, and local communities all affect whether patients and families can make informed decisions.

Living donationOften accepted for paired or regenerative organs when harm to the donor is minimized.
Deceased donationMore contested because death determination, consent, dignity, and burial timing matter.
Community educationMosques and trusted local leaders can address uncertainty and misinformation.
Clinical counselingNurses and physicians help families navigate medical facts alongside religious concerns.

57 nations

The paper cites 57 predominantly Islamic nations and describes uneven transplant infrastructure, funding, public education, and scholarly support.

70% understood permission

In a cited study of 251 Muslim American physicians, 70% reported that Islamic bioethics and law permit organ donation.

30% registered donors

In the same physician survey, only 30% reported being registered as organ donors, illustrating the gap between knowledge and action.

02 / Reproductive methods

Alternative reproduction within Islam

The second paper examines assisted reproductive technology through marriage, family formation, progeny, and lineage. Technologies are not evaluated as one group: the origin of gametes, the role of third parties, timing, purpose, and risks all matter.

Marriage and lineage

The paper describes infertility treatment as compatible with the importance Islam places on family formation, while emphasizing that assisted reproduction usually remains bounded by marriage and the preservation of lineage.

  • IVF using the married couple's own sperm and ovum is generally presented as permissible.
  • Third-party sperm, egg, embryo, or uterus involvement raises lineage questions.
  • Sunni and Shia interpretations can differ, especially for gamete donation.

Technology is assessed by purpose

The paper distinguishes treatment from enhancement. Preimplantation genetic diagnosis may be preferred when it reduces the need for later pregnancy termination. Somatic gene therapy is treated differently from heritable germline editing.

Safety and efficacy remain central to the discussion of germline editing. The paper argues that preventing harm and preserving life should guide any future consideration.

Synthesis

What the two papers share

The topics are different, but the reasoning pattern is consistent: technology is not evaluated in isolation. Its medical purpose, risks, social context, and relationship to core ethical commitments all shape the analysis.

Knowledge before action

Both papers emphasize informed decision-making. Clinicians and religious leaders serve as trusted interpreters when patients face unfamiliar technologies.

Benefit is conditional

A medical benefit does not automatically settle every question. Consent, harm, dignity, lineage, timing, and circumstance still matter.

Context changes practice

Scholarly interpretations, sectarian differences, national laws, infrastructure, and community knowledge all affect real-world access and uptake.
