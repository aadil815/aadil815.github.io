Research Focus

CTE is a neurodegenerative tauopathy associated with exposure to repetitive head impacts, including concussive and nonconcussive impacts. Its defining lesion is a distinctive accumulation of phosphorylated tau around small blood vessels at the depths of cortical sulci.

CTE can currently be definitively diagnosed only through postmortem neuropathological examination. Clinical symptoms in living patients require careful differential diagnosis.

01 / Background

From boxing observations to a modern neuropathology

The source papers traced a century of growing attention to long-term brain injury in athletes. Modern consensus criteria now distinguish CTE from other tauopathies through the location and distribution of its pathology.

02 / Mechanism

Why tau matters

Tau normally supports the microtubule structure of neurons. In CTE, abnormal phosphorylated tau aggregates appear in a characteristic perivascular and sulcal pattern that differs from other neurodegenerative diseases.

A distinct diagnostic pattern

The critical finding is not simply the presence of tau. It is the lesion pattern: p-tau aggregates in neurons, with or without glial p-tau, around a small blood vessel at the depth of a cortical sulcus.

  • Exposure can include repeated subconcussive impacts.
  • Pathology frequently involves frontal and temporal regions as disease severity increases.
  • CTE remains a pathological diagnosis; the corresponding clinical research syndrome is called traumatic encephalopathy syndrome (TES).
03 / Neuropathology

Progression across the brain

The original presentations emphasized a four-stage model. The stages describe increasing anatomical distribution and burden of p-tau pathology, not a simple symptom checklist for diagnosing a living person.

Stage I

Focal lesions

Isolated perivascular lesions typically emerge at sulcal depths in frontal or temporal cortex.

Stage II

Multiple cortical sites

Additional lesions appear across frontal, temporal, and parietal cortices with early spread.

Stage III

Widespread involvement

Pathology extends across cortical and medial temporal structures, including the hippocampus and amygdala.

Stage IV

Severe distribution

Dense cortical pathology and neurodegeneration affect additional deep brain and brainstem structures.

04 / Exposure & symptoms

The clinical picture is variable

The papers described mood, behavior, cognition, and motor changes. Current research remains careful: these symptoms are not specific to CTE, and symptoms alone cannot confirm the underlying pathology.

Exposure context

CTE has been reported among people with repeated head-impact exposure, including participants in contact sports and military activities.

Possible presentation

Research descriptions include problems with mood, behavior, memory, executive function, and later-life cognition, with substantial individual variation.

Living-patient research

Researchers are studying biomarkers, neuroimaging, and TES criteria. These tools do not yet replace neuropathological confirmation.

05 / Evidence

Interpreting the 2017 football study

The presentation highlighted the striking 110-of-111 result. That statistic is important, but it must be interpreted within the design of the study.

What the study found

In a 2017 JAMA case series of donated brains from deceased football players, researchers diagnosed CTE in 177 of 202 players overall, including 110 of 111 former NFL players.

What it does not establish

The brain-bank sample was not designed to estimate CTE prevalence in all football players. Donation and selection factors mean the result should not be read as “99% of NFL players develop CTE.”

Separate case study

Aaron Hernandez

The 2020 papers developed this material most fully through a review of Killer Inside: The Mind of Aaron Hernandez. It is presented separately because an individual case should not be treated as the definition of CTE.

Neuropathological finding

After Hernandez died at age 27, Boston University researchers reported Stage III CTE. The findings included substantial disease burden for his age and damage affecting regions associated with memory, judgment, and behavior.

  • The source papers connect the case to a history of football exposure from youth through the NFL.
  • The case illustrates why young-age exposure and cumulative impacts remain important research questions.
  • The diagnosis was made postmortem, after brain donation and examination.
CTE is relevant clinical context, but neuropathology alone cannot explain or excuse a person’s actions. The Hernandez case also involves psychosocial history, substance use, and other factors that should not be collapsed into a single cause.
06 / Research direction

What comes next

The consistent question across the papers is how to identify risk earlier and reduce exposure before irreversible pathology develops.

Prevention

Reduce unnecessary repetitive head impacts and improve recognition, reporting, and management of head injury.

Biomarkers

Develop and validate imaging and fluid biomarkers that can support research in living participants.

Clinical precision

Distinguish pathology, clinical syndrome, and co-occurring conditions so that symptoms receive appropriate care.
